# Archetype 12: Draft a bill (for a willing sponsor)

> Statutory text drafted for a sponsor who'll introduce it.

## Why this archetype exists

Most "AI policy work" stops at the memo or the op-ed — the recommendation
to a decision-maker. A bill is the decision itself, written down. When
the legislative gap is real, the sponsor is named, and the timing is
right, drafting the bill is the single highest-leverage move on this
menu: the artifact you produce *is the law* if it passes.

It is also the hardest archetype to ship. The bar is meaningfully higher
than for any of the others — it requires three different kinds of
expertise at once, a willing sponsor who'll spend political capital, and
the patience to navigate a months-long session where the bill may not
move on your timeline. Most cohort members should not pick this one.
The "When NOT to pick this" section below is heavier than usual for a
reason.

## When to pick this

- **You have a named, willing sponsor.** A real legislator or
  legislator's office that has agreed to introduce the bill, not a
  legislator you hope to recruit. The sponsor is the gating constraint.
- **You can name the legislative gap in one sentence**, and you've
  confirmed it can't be solved by regulation or agency action. A
  rulemaking comment (#6) is faster and often more leverage; pick this
  only if the fix actually requires statute.
- **You have 12–24 weeks of capacity.** Sessions are slow. The bill
  might not move on your timeline. If you need signal before then, pick
  a faster archetype.
- **You can read and write legislative language** (sections,
  subsections, definitions, repealers, effective dates) — or you have
  a partner (legislative counsel, lobbyist, advocacy org staffer) who
  can. Bill text has its own conventions; lay-prose policy writing
  doesn't translate directly.
- **You have a substantive brief** — a memo (#3), landscape doc (#4),
  or tracker (#1) — that frames the gap. The bill comes *from* that
  brief; it's not the place to figure out what you think.

## When NOT to pick this

- **You don't have a sponsor.** This is the most common failure mode.
  Bills with no sponsor don't get introduced; "I drafted this and I
  hope someone picks it up" is wasted text. Find the sponsor first, or
  partner with an advocacy organization that has the relationships.
- **The fix is regulatory, not legislative.** Most AI-policy gaps are
  agency-level — guidance documents, NIST framework updates, executive
  orders, rulemakings. Use archetype #6 (public comment) if there's an
  open window. Legislation is heavier machinery for a narrower set of
  problems.
- **You're a 0–3 on the clarity scale at the top of `PLAYBOOK.md`.**
  Bills require a clear policy theory of change. If you can't write
  the memo (#3) that the bill would implement, you're not ready. Do
  the memo first; the right vehicle (bill, regulation, op-ed) becomes
  clearer.
- **You haven't worked in or near a legislative office before.** Bill
  drafting requires knowing the conventions of statute-writing and the
  internal politics of how legislative offices schedule and prioritize.
  If you don't, partner with someone who does, or pick a different
  archetype.
- **You're picking this because it sounds the most ambitious.** Bills
  are extraordinary leverage *only if* they pass or substantively shape
  the conversation around the next bill. The failure mode is filing a
  "messaging bill" that dies in committee and produces nothing — the
  sponsor's office spent capital, you spent months, and the policy
  landscape didn't move.

## What v1 looks like

- A **bill drafted** in proper legislative form (sections,
  subsections, definitions, repealer / sunset clauses, effective date).
- A **named sponsor** who has agreed to file it (not a hoped-for
  sponsor).
- The **bill filed** — introduced into the chamber under a real bill
  number.
- A **committee assignment** (the bill referred to a committee).
- A **one-page brief** for the sponsor's office on the bill's argument,
  the named constituencies it serves, and the likely opposition.

You're done with v1 when the bill is filed and assigned to committee.
Done well when it gets a hearing. Even better when it's voted out of
committee. Best case: passes and is signed.

## What v2/v3 looks like

- A second bill in a follow-on session refining the first (most AI
  policy needs multiple bills to land; v1 is rarely the final form).
- A **regulatory follow-through** — once the bill passes, the agencies
  named in it produce rulemaking, which is where archetype #6 (public
  comment) becomes the next move.
- A **memo to the sponsor's office** on lessons from v1, recommending
  the next 1–2 bills in the same area.

## Where credibility comes from

Bill-drafting credibility comes from **three places at once**:

1. **Substantive policy expertise** — you understand the underlying
   problem and the existing legal landscape.
2. **Legislative-process knowledge** — you understand how bills move,
   what conventions matter, and who the gatekeepers are.
3. **The sponsor's political capital** — the bill moves because a
   legislator with capital chooses to spend it on this.

You need access to all three. Most cohort members have at most one. If
you have only (1), the partner you need is someone with (2) and (3) —
typically an advocacy org's policy director, a former staffer, or
legislative counsel themselves.

## Example in the wild

**IL HB5760**, the AI Work Transition Commission Act, sponsored by
Kimberly du Buclet (D) in the 104th Illinois General Assembly.
Introduced April 30, 2026; referred to Rules Committee May 5, 2026.
Establishes a state commission to assess the impact of generative and
frontier AI systems on Illinois industries and workers, and to
recommend updates to workforce-development, education, apprenticeship,
unemployment, and income-support programs.

## How I'd start if I were you (week 1)

1. **Confirm the fix is legislative, not regulatory.** Talk to
   legislative counsel or a former staffer. If a state agency could do
   this with existing authority via rulemaking, use #6 instead.
2. **Identify the sponsor.** Who in the chamber cares about this issue?
   Whose committees would the bill go through? Who would author it
   willingly versus need convincing?
3. **Reach out informally first.** Coffee with the sponsor's staffer,
   not a 10-page memo. Pitch the *gap*, not the bill text. The bill is
   the staffer's draft after you've sold them on the gap.
4. **Draft the substantive memo (archetype #3) first.** The memo to
   the sponsor's office is what makes the case. The bill draft comes
   from the memo, not the other way around.

## Common failure modes

- **Drafting without a sponsor.** Unfiled text is not v1.
- **Treating "I drafted this" as the artifact.** The artifact is the
  bill being *filed*. A drafted-but-never-introduced bill is a memo
  with worse formatting.
- **Underestimating the multi-stakeholder dance.** Sponsor, legislative
  counsel, committee staff, opposing lobbyists, advocacy orgs, the
  Governor's office (for state-level) — all have to be navigated. The
  drafting is maybe 20% of the work.
- **Designing a "messaging bill" that doesn't move.** If the sponsor
  won't fight for the bill in committee, the bill is a press release.
  Test the sponsor's commitment before you commit your months.
- **Picking the wrong jurisdiction.** State bills move faster but
  reach less; federal bills reach more but rarely move. Decide which
  trade-off you're willing to make before you start.

## Combinations

- **#1 (tracker) → #12 (bill)** — the tracker surfaces the gap (no
  existing statute on X); the bill fills it. Best when you can point
  at the missing row in your own tracker.
- **#3 (memo) → #12 (bill)** — the memo is the brief the sponsor's
  office reads. The bill is the staffer's draft after the sponsor
  agrees with the memo.
- **#12 (bill) → #6 (public comment)** — once the bill passes, the
  agencies named in it produce rulemaking. Your next move is comments
  on that rulemaking.
