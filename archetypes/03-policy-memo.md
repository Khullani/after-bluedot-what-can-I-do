# Archetype 3: Policy memo for a specific stakeholder

> One named decision-maker. One decision they're about to make. Two pages —
> or, in the staff-analysis variant, five.

## Two variants

This archetype comes in two shapes. Pick the one that matches your audience.

- **Recommendation memo** (2 pages, [`one-pager.md`](../templates/one-pager.md)).
  The default. One decision-maker, one decision, recommendation up front.
- **Staff bill analysis** (5–8 pages,
  [`staff-bill-analysis.md`](../templates/staff-bill-analysis.md)). Same
  archetype, different audience: committee staff and members who need to
  read a complex bill section by section before they can act on a
  recommendation. The template ends in a recommendation section, so it
  does the advocacy work the recommendation memo does — just with the
  structural walk-through ahead of it.

When in doubt, write the 2-page memo. The staff analysis is for cases
where the 2-page memo would leave too many open questions about what's
actually in the bill.

## Why this archetype exists

Most "AI policy" writing aims at no one in particular and is read by no one
in particular. A policy memo aimed at a specific stakeholder, delivered
before a specific decision, is the single highest-leverage thing on this
menu when you can pull off the targeting. Legislators, agency staff,
chiefs of staff, and CTOs all read memos. They do not read white papers.

This archetype is what credibility looks like in policy work: not the
length of your CV, but the fact that a named person can say "I read your
memo and it changed how I thought about X."

## When to pick this

- You can name **the** decision-maker — not "policymakers," not "the
  governor's office," a person.
- You can name the decision: a vote, a rulemaking, a procurement, a
  testimony, a public statement.
- You have a credible route to delivery — you know them, or you know
  someone who can put the memo in front of them, or there's a public
  comment period that puts it in front of them by default.
- You can finish a 2-page draft in 10–20 hours of work spread over 2–4
  weeks.

## When NOT to pick this

- You don't have a route to delivery. A great memo nobody reads is
  worse than a mediocre memo delivered. If you can't name the route,
  pick #5 (op-ed) or #6 (public comment) instead — both have delivery
  built in.
- You're picking the stakeholder because they're famous rather than
  because they're making a decision you can influence.
- You'd write a 20-page paper. Memos are 2 pages. If you can't say it
  in 2 pages, you don't know it well enough yet.

## What v1 looks like

A two-page memo with this structure:

1. **TL;DR** (3–5 lines): the recommendation, in plain language.
2. **The decision** (1 paragraph): what they're being asked to decide,
   when, with what stakes.
3. **The recommendation** (1 paragraph): what you're recommending,
   crisply.
4. **Why** (3–5 bullets): the reasoning, with evidence and citations.
5. **What could go wrong** (2–3 bullets): the strongest counterarguments,
   acknowledged honestly.
6. **The ask** (1 paragraph): what specifically you want them to do,
   and by when.

You're done with v1 when you've sent it to the stakeholder (or to the
person who will hand it to them) and gotten an acknowledgement.

## What v2/v3 looks like

- A short op-ed (archetype #5) that makes the public case for the same
  recommendation.
- A 6-page longer version for the stakeholder's staff to brief them on.
- A coalition memo: same recommendation, co-signed by 4–6 other
  practitioners with their own credibility.

## Example in the wild

I don't have a public example of mine — memos and staff bill analyses
delivered to sponsors and committee staff are usually working documents
and stay private through active legislative negotiation. The shape to
imitate is the kind of one-pager congressional staff actually read:
tight, recommendation up front, opinion declared rather than hedged.

## How I'd start if I were you (week 1)

1. **Write the TL;DR first**, in one sitting, before any research. If
   you can't write it now, you don't have a memo yet — you have a
   research project. Be honest.
2. **Confirm the delivery route.** Email the contact who will hand it
   over and confirm they're game. If they're not, you're writing into
   the void.
3. **Pick the deadline.** Memos without a deadline don't ship. Decide
   the date you'll send it before you start writing.
4. **Outline the 5 bullets under "Why."** If you can't fill them in
   from what you already know, the recommendation isn't ready.

## Common failure modes

- **Hedging the recommendation.** "Policymakers should consider X" is
  not a recommendation. "Senator Cunningham should vote yes on HB1234"
  is. Stake a claim or don't write the memo.
- **Burying the lede.** If your TL;DR doesn't say what you want them
  to do, in the first sentence, rewrite it.
- **Treating it like academic writing.** No literature review, no
  hedged tone, no methodology section. Plain English, opinion declared,
  evidence cited inline.
