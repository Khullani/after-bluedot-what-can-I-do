# Archetype 14: Networking events

> Show up at the right events with named-person targets and structured pre-work; leave with N qualified follow-ups.

## Why this archetype exists

Conferences, meetups, EAG, virtual townhalls, regional AI policy
gatherings — most people attend reactively and leave with business
cards and tweets and not much else. The version that actually compounds
is the one where you arrive with a list of 3–5 named people you want to
meet, a specific reason for each, and a structured plan for the
follow-up.

Events are also where you **promote your work** — a memo, a landscape
doc, a tracker, a Substack post — to people who can't find it any
other way. Visibility doesn't happen in private; it happens in the room
and in the follow-up.

## When to pick this

**Best fit for:** any of the four BlueDot courses (FAIG, AGIS, BIOS, TAIS).

- There's a **relevant event in your calendar window** — EAG, a
  research workshop, a virtual meetup, a townhall for a rationality
  programme, a regional AI policy conference.
- You can name **3–5 people** who'll be there that you'd want to meet,
  with a specific reason each.
- You have **something to point at** when introducing yourself — even a
  draft Substack post counts.
- You can spare the time for the event *plus* the 48-hour follow-up
  window.

## When NOT to pick this

- **You're attending out of FOMO.** "Important people will be there"
  isn't a plan; "I want to meet X about Y" is.
- **You have nothing to show.** The introduction "I'm interested in AI
  policy" doesn't compound; "I wrote a memo on X and want to know if
  I'm wrong about Y" does. Ship one of the writing archetypes first.
- **You'd default to the people you already know.** The point is to
  compound the network, not refresh it.
- **You'd skip the follow-up.** Without it, the event is entertainment.

## What v1 looks like

For each event:

1. **Pre-event list** — 3–5 named-person targets, with a one-sentence
   reason per person. Plus your one-line pitch ("I write a Substack on
   X / facilitate a BlueDot cohort / shipped a tracker on Y") refined
   to fit the audience.
2. **At the event** — meet the named targets; share what you're working
   on; ask specifically what they're working on; *write down what you
   heard* before you leave.
3. **48-hour follow-up** — personal email to each person you actually
   talked to, naming one specific thing from the conversation and one
   specific next step.
4. **A count of qualified follow-ups** — at least 5 personal emails
   sent, at least 2 of those resulting in scheduled 1:1s (which feeds
   archetype #13).
5. **Promoting your work** — at least 3 people leave the event with a
   link to something you wrote or built. "Promotion" here isn't
   self-marketing; it's letting your existing work do the talking.

You're done with v1 when 2 events have run this loop, or 1 event has
gone all the way through (named targets met → follow-ups sent → 1:1s
scheduled).

## What to do after v1

- **Pick the next event by overlap with the people you wish you'd kept
  talking to.** Events are most useful when they reconnect rather than
  introduce.
- **Organize the next one if you can't find a good one.** Hosting a
  townhall, a virtual meetup for your local AI safety cohort, or a
  panel at a larger conference compounds further than attending does —
  but only after you've attended a few first.
- **Publish a short post** (archetype #11) summarizing what you
  learned. People you met will share it; people you didn't meet will
  find you.

## How I'd start if I were you (week 1)

1. **Pick the next event in your calendar window.** EAG, your local AI
   safety meetup, a virtual townhall for a rationality programme, a
   regional policy conference — anything with the right people.
2. **Build the named-targets list** before you register. If you can't
   name 3, the event isn't the right one yet.
3. **Refine your one-line pitch.** What you're working on, in 15
   words, ending in something they can engage with.
4. **Block the 48-hour follow-up window** on your calendar before the
   event starts. The follow-up is the artifact; protect the time
   before the event eats it.

## Common failure modes

- **Going without a list.** You'll talk to whoever's nearest, miss the
  people you came for, and leave with no follow-ups.
- **Treating the meeting as the artifact.** The artifact is the
  follow-up email. Without it, you've done expensive networking.
- **Performing the version of yourself you think the audience wants.**
  Better to be specific about what you're actually working on, even if
  it sounds smaller; that's what gives the conversation traction.
- **Skipping promotion.** If nobody at the event leaves with a link to
  something you wrote, the event didn't change your visibility.
