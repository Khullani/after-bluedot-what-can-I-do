# Archetype 5: Op-ed / public writing

> One published piece in an outlet your stakeholders read.

## Why this archetype exists

A Substack with twelve subscribers is not public writing. A piece in the
Chicago Tribune, Politico, Lawfare, or your state's policy outlet is. The
gap between those two — between "I wrote about it" and "I placed it" —
is mostly about treating the op-ed as a delivery mechanism for one
specific argument to one specific reader, not a venue for your views.

When done right, one placed op-ed beats ten Substack posts. Editors are
gatekeepers, and the act of clearing the gate is part of the value: it
signals to readers that someone independent thought this argument was
worth their attention.

## When to pick this

**Best fit for:** any of the four BlueDot courses (FAIG, AGIS, BIOS, TAIS).

- You have **one** sharp argument. Not three. One.
- You can name the outlet and, ideally, the specific section editor.
- You have a hook — a recent event, a bill, a court ruling — that gives
  the editor a reason to publish *this week*.
- You can write 700–900 words in plain English without academic hedging.
- You have 5–15 hours total over 3–6 weeks (writing is fast, placing is
  slow).

## When NOT to pick this

- Your argument is "AI is important and we should think about it
  carefully." That's not an argument, that's a vibe. Find a real
  claim or pick a different archetype.
- You want to write because you like writing. Pick #4 (landscape doc)
  — it tolerates longer-form, less-targeted writing.
- You can't name the outlet. You'll spend three weeks shopping a piece
  nobody picks up. Pick the outlet first, write to fit it second.

## What v1 looks like

A 700–900 word piece, placed in a named outlet, with this structure:

1. **The hook** (1 paragraph): the news event or moment that makes
   this timely.
2. **The stakes** (1–2 sentences): why a non-expert reader should care.
3. **The argument** (3 paragraphs): your claim, in three beats, with
   one piece of evidence each.
4. **The strongest objection** (1 paragraph): acknowledged honestly,
   then addressed.
5. **The call** (1 paragraph): what should happen next, and who should
   do it.

You're done when it's published. Not when it's drafted, submitted, or
accepted — published.

## What v2/v3 looks like

- A second op-ed in a different outlet making a complementary point.
- A longer companion piece on your own site or a landscape doc
  (archetype #4) that the op-ed links to.
- An invitation to a panel or a podcast based on the op-ed (which can
  seed archetype #7).

## Example in the wild

[`examples/05-op-ed.md`](../examples/05-op-ed.md) —
**"AI legislation must focus on biggest risks"** in *Crain's Chicago
Business* (Commentary section, March 30, 2026), arguing that Illinois
should pass HB 4705 / SB 3261 because surgically targeting only the
largest AI companies best balances public safety with innovation. Aimed
at Illinois state legislators in Springfield. Byline: "Khullani
Abdullahi is CEO of Techne AI in Chicago."

Other external examples worth studying: Lawfare's AI policy section,
Tech Policy Press, the op-ed pages of state-level papers. Notice how
short and concrete the placed pieces are compared to similar arguments
on Substack.

## How I'd start if I were you (week 1)

1. **Pick the argument and the outlet on the same day.** Not one
   without the other. Reverse-engineer the argument to fit the
   outlet's voice and audience.
2. **Find the section editor's email.** Most outlets list a tipline
   or section editor. The piece goes nowhere without that contact.
3. **Write the pitch first**, not the piece. A 200-word pitch with
   the hook, the argument, and your bona fides. Send it before
   you draft.
4. **Draft on a deadline.** 700–900 words, plain English. Cut every
   "I would argue that" and every "it is important to note." Be
   willing to be wrong out loud.

## Common failure modes

- **Three arguments in one op-ed.** Editors reject these. Pick one.
- **Academic register.** "This paper argues that…" — no. "We should
  do X because Y" — yes.
- **No hook.** Editors run op-eds because something happened. If
  nothing happened, your piece won't run. Wait for the next event
  in your area, or pick a different archetype.
- **Submitting and ghosting.** If you haven't heard back in 5 business
  days, follow up. If radio silence at 10 days, withdraw and re-pitch
  to the next outlet on your list.
