# Archetype 13: 1:1 outreach

> Cold emails and scheduled meetings with a named list of 8–12 people you want feedback or visibility from.

## Why this archetype exists

Opportunities don't find you until you make yourself, your ideas, and
your capabilities visible. The fastest way to do that — faster than
writing a landscape doc, more direct than attending an event — is to
deliberately reach the people you most want to reach, one at a time,
with a specific ask.

Cold emails work. Scheduled 1:1s work. They don't feel like "doing the
work" because they look like email. But cohort members who treated
outreach as an archetype — building a real list, sending specific asks,
following up — have, without exception, gotten more interesting
opportunities than the ones who waited for visibility to come find
them.

This is also the archetype you can run **while you wait for decisions**
on jobs (#9) or fellowships (#10). The thing you ship while you wait
is often what makes the next decision easier to win.

## When to pick this

**Best fit for:** any of the four BlueDot courses (FAIG, AGIS, BIOS, TAIS).

- You have **prior work to point at** — a memo, an op-ed, a tracker, a
  landscape doc, a technical writeup, a course-reflection Substack post.
  Outreach without something to show is a generic introduction.
- You can name **8–12 specific people** who'd be valuable to talk to,
  with at least the rough reason for each (feedback on X, learn about
  Y, collaborate on Z).
- You have **6 weeks** for the cadence: drafting, sending, tracking,
  following up.
- You're comfortable being told no — most outreach gets ignored. The
  20% that converts is the whole game.

## When NOT to pick this

- **You have nothing to show yet.** Send the cold email *after* you
  ship one of the writing or action archetypes. The artifact is what
  gives the email a reason to exist.
- **You're hoping for "introductions" rather than substantive
  responses.** Pick the people whose own work you've already engaged
  with; cold-emailing strangers for general advice is high-volume,
  low-signal.
- **You'd treat any non-response as personal failure.** The base rate
  for cold outreach is 70–80% non-response even when the email is
  excellent.
- **You'd send the same email to 50 people.** Generic outreach destroys
  your name on those lists.

## What v1 looks like

A focused 6-week outreach campaign:

1. **A named list of 8–12 targets**, with a one-sentence reason per
   person (feedback on X / learn how Y works / explore Z collaboration).
2. **Personalized emails sent to all of them** — different subject
   lines, different opening hooks, different specific asks. Boilerplate
   is detectable; specificity isn't.
3. **3 meetings booked**, with explicit ask and agenda in the calendar
   invite.
4. **Structured notes per meeting** — what was asked, what you learned,
   what they suggested, what you committed to. Saved somewhere you can
   reference 12 months later.
5. **Follow-up emails within 48 hours** of each meeting, naming a
   specific next step.

You're done with v1 when 3 meetings have happened and follow-ups are
sent.

## What to do after v1

- **Turn the 3 most useful meetings into ongoing 1:1 cadences.**
  Quarterly check-ins with three people who get your work is more
  valuable than 30 first-time introductions.
- **Publish what you learned** (archetype #11). The synthesis post is
  something the people you talked to will share, which compounds your
  visibility further.
- **Identify the next 5 targets** from connections the first cohort
  introduced you to. Outreach compounds best when each round seeds the
  next.

## How I'd start if I were you (week 1)

1. **Build the list.** Spreadsheet with name, affiliation, why you want
   to talk to them, the specific artifact of yours that's relevant. If
   you can't fill the "why" column, that person isn't a target yet.
2. **Write one email, fully**, to the highest-leverage person on your
   list. Reverse-engineer the rest of your template from that one.
   *Don't* write a template first — the template comes after you've
   written one that works.
3. **Send the first three emails by end of week.** Cadence kills you if
   you wait for perfect. Three emails in week one beats twelve in week
   six.
4. **Set up a tracking sheet** — sent / replied / scheduled / met /
   followed-up. Without it, the campaign disappears into your inbox by
   week three.

## Common failure modes

- **Generic outreach to large lists.** Twelve specific, personalized
  emails outperform a hundred generic ones, and don't burn your name in
  the process.
- **No artifact to point at.** "I'd love to learn more about your work"
  is dead on arrival. Lead with "I read X and thought Y; I wrote Z and
  want to know if I'm right about [specific thing]."
- **No follow-up.** The follow-up email after a meeting is where the
  relationship actually starts. People who skip it stay strangers to
  people they've met.
- **Treating the meeting as the goal.** The meeting is the start. The
  artifact you ship *because* of the meeting (a revised memo, a new
  connection, a specific intro) is the actual output.
