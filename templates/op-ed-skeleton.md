# [Op-Ed Title — Sharp, Specific, 6–12 Words]

> Target: 700–900 words. Plain English. One argument. No academic register.

## The hook (1 paragraph, 60–100 words)

Open with the news event, court ruling, bill, or moment that makes this
piece timely *this week*. Editors run op-eds because something happened.
Name that something in the first sentence.

> Example opener: "When the [agency / legislature / company] announced
> [thing] last [day], it set [stake]. What [decision-maker] does next
> will determine [consequence]."

## The stakes (1–2 sentences)

Why does a non-expert reader care? The "so what" line. If you can't write
this, your reader won't finish the piece.

## The argument (3 paragraphs, 3 beats)

### Beat one — the claim

State your central claim plainly. Not "I would argue that" — "X is true,
and here's why."

### Beat two — the evidence

One concrete piece of evidence. A number, an example, a quote. Not three.
One.

### Beat three — the implication

Why does the claim matter for the decision the reader cares about? Tie it
back to the hook.

## The strongest objection (1 paragraph)

Acknowledge the best counterargument. Not a strawman — the real one. Then
address it. This is the paragraph that separates op-eds from advocacy
posts. Editors weight this heavily.

## The call (1 paragraph)

What should happen next, and who should do it? Name the actor. "Congress
should…" is weak. "The Senate Judiciary Committee should…" is stronger.
"Chairman Durbin should…" is strongest.

---

## Pre-submit checklist

- [ ] One argument, not three.
- [ ] Hook ties to a specific news event in the past 1–2 weeks.
- [ ] Word count between 700 and 900.
- [ ] Cut every "I would argue that," "it is important to note,"
      "we must remember that."
- [ ] One named call-to-action at the end.
- [ ] Tagline / bio is two lines, max.
- [ ] You can name the section editor at the target outlet.
